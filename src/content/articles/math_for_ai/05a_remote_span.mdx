<Journey title="Span · 5.1">

<Step>

Fahim still remembers the library machine: the thick book on boats and paddy jumped to number two in a search for fish, just because its arrow was long. But the holidays are over, and Fahim's family is moving house. In the new flat, it turns out this time the question isn't about measuring likeness at all.

The new flat is still empty. There's only the squares of the floor tiles and a few boxes. Ammu chalked four marks on the floor, for where each thing will go.

Shiku's remote broke in the packing. The toy shop downstairs has four kinds of remote, each with two buttons, and Fahim has money for one. From the door corner, Shiku has to reach all four marks.

Nasib heard and said, “With two buttons you can go anywhere.” The shop closes at Maghrib.

<MovingDay story />

<RemoteShelf />

<Then>

Bet sealed. The four remotes look almost the same, two buttons each. The only difference is the numbers written on the buttons. Those numbers will decide where Shiku can go, and where he can't.

<ShelfFour />

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

The cheapest remote in the shop has only one button, $v = (1, 1)$. Fahim thought, let's try this one first. The button can be pressed forwards, and backwards too, and if you stop half-way, that's half a press.

<OneButton />

<Then>

Wherever Shiku stopped, the dots sat on one straight line. Remember the sherbet in 3.2? Multiply a card by any number you like, and its tip stays on that one line. Same here, and the line passes right through the door.

Everywhere one button can take you, all together, is that line. It has a name: **span**. Written down, it's $\text{span}\{v\}$.

<LineNamed />

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

Now remote A, the old kind. One button goes one tile east, $e_1 = (1, 0)$, and the other one tile north, $e_2 = (0, 1)$. In 3.3, Shiku had exactly this remote.

<OldRemote />

<Then>

Noticed? To reach each mark, the two numbers you pressed were the mark's own two numbers. The almirah is at $(3, 5)$, so $e_1$ three times and $e_2$ five times.

<AlmirahBuild />

Name any tile on the floor, and that tile's two numbers are the press counts. So remote A's span is the whole floor. Nasib's rule survives, for now.

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

Remote B's two buttons: $u = (1, 1)$ and $v = (2, 2)$. Two different pairs of numbers, so by Nasib's count this one should go everywhere too.

<TwinButtons />

<Then>

The table came easily. But try the almirah as many times as you like, and Shiku lands on that one slanted line.

The reason is plain once you look at the two buttons. $(2, 2)$ is just $(1, 1)$ twice, so pressing v once is the same as pressing u twice. So there are two buttons, but only one direction.

<SameDirection />

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

Let's check with numbers why the almirah can't be reached at all. It's the treasure sum from 3.1 again: first slot with first slot, second with second, each stays in its own slot.

Now let's write, step by step, where Shiku stands after α presses of u and β presses of v. α and β can be anything, even minus.

<SlotsSame />

<Then>

The two slots came out exactly the same, $\alpha + 2\beta$. Whatever α and β you put in, whatever is in the first slot is exactly what's in the second.

The almirah wants 3 in the first slot and 5 in the second, two different numbers. But this remote can't put different numbers in the two slots at all. So the almirah isn't hard. The almirah is impossible. **Nasib's rule loses for the first time.**

<TwinSlots />

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

The third remote on the shelf, C. Its two buttons look the messiest: $u = (1, 2)$ and $v = (2, 5)$. Will it get stuck like B?

<MessyRemote />

<Then>

First u five times, and Shiku went up to $(5, 10)$, with the almirah left far below. Then v once, backwards, and $(5, 10) - (2, 5) = (3, 5)$. Right on the almirah's tile.

Messy to look at, but u and v push two different ways. Neither one is a multiple of the other. That's all it takes. Go a little past, then step back, and you can land on any tile.

<BackStep />

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

Three of the four done, and Maghrib is coming. The shopkeeper said every remote has a toggle. Press it, and the floor lights up in colour: everywhere that remote can reach.

<PaintReach />

<Then>

Take every way a remote's buttons can be pressed, and put all those spots together. That's the **span** of those buttons. For two buttons, we write $\text{span}\{u, v\}$.

The paint showed it: a span is sometimes a line, sometimes the whole floor. Which one isn't decided by how many buttons there are. It's decided by which ways the buttons push.

<ThreeSpans />

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

But one thing was the same in all four painted pictures. The door corner fell inside the paint every time.

<DoorEveryTime story />

<Check
  question="Take any remote at all. Will the door always be inside its span?"
  options={[
    "No, it depends on which buttons it has.",
    "Only if it has two buttons.",
    "Yes, always.",
  ]}
  answer={2}
  hint="If you don't press a single button, where is Shiku standing?"
  praise="Pressing nothing is a combination too."
/>

<Then>

Every button pressed zero times: that's a combination too, $0 \cdot u + 0 \cdot v = (0, 0)$. That's the door. So whatever the buttons are, the door never falls outside the span.

So a span is never empty. However bad the remote, it reaches at least one place: its own door.

<DoorInside />

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

The shopkeeper took four more remotes down from the back of the shelf. Now it's your turn: before any paint, say which one's span is a line and which one's is the whole floor.

<YourRemotes />

<Then>

Remote 3 had a $(0, 0)$ button. Press it and Shiku doesn't move a hair, so having it and not having it are the same. That leaves one button in hand, which means one line.

<DeadButton />

See? Counting buttons tells you nothing. Two buttons can give a line, and two buttons can also give the whole floor.

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

## Try it

One remote has only the $(2, 0)$ button. Another has both, $(2, 0)$ and $(-5, 0)$. Is their span the same?

<WallPick />

<Then>

The two spans are the same. $(-5, 0)$ is $-2.5$ times $(2, 0)$, so it runs along that same wall, only the other way. And the other way, you could already get by pressing the first button in minus.

Those who picked “the whole floor” counted the buttons. There really are two buttons, but only one direction. So a span isn't always the whole floor, not even with two buttons.

<WallLine />

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

## The end! Fahim buys remote C

Just before Maghrib, Fahim came out of the shop holding that messy-looking remote C. A would have worked too, but C was cheaper.

<RemoteBought story />

Open the four remotes' results one by one, and check them against your bet.

<Then>

Nasib's rule lost twice. B and D had two buttons each, yet both got stuck on one line. The rule that survived: **counting buttons gets you nothing; look at how far they reach.** And “how far they reach” is called span.
