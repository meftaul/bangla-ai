<Step>

Moving week, day two. Yesterday's four remotes are still on the table, the twin remote among them. Both buttons push along the same slanted line, so one line is the whole floor it can cover. Keep that in mind about directions — because today the dalal is coming back, and this time he's carrying the rent khata.

Fahim's family is flat-hunting, and it's down to two. Same size, same floor, same side of town — but flat B has one extra bathroom, and it costs 4000 taka more a month. So exactly one question matters now: what is one bathroom worth?

The dalal's rent app should know. It reads the ledger, it quotes a price per bathroom. This morning it said one thing. This evening — after one more flat went into the ledger — it said another.

The trouble is hiding somewhere in the khata itself, and there's one way out: read the thing, column by column. By the last screen you'll know which sealed answer wins — and why.

<Then>

Six numbers on that page, and only three facts. Whatever the sq m column could tell Abbu, the sq ft column had already told him — divide by 10.76 and you can rebuild the new column exactly, row after row. A column like that is just an old column in a new coat. But page two's trick is subtler.

<Step>

Page two is the flats themselves: beds, baths, total rooms, rent. Samin squints at the total column. It isn't a copy of bed, it isn't a copy of bath — it changes from flat to flat like something that knows things. So is it a new fact?

<TotalColumn />

<Then>

Total = bed + bath, on every single row. Not a copy — but built out of the other two, to the last room. That's a subtler coat than page one's: this column does change, it does look busy. Still, ask it one question and it has no answer of its own — whatever it says, bed and bath had already decided. From here on, a column like this gets its name: an **extra column**.

</Then>

<Step>

Back in 3.3, the tiffin recipe had exactly two moves: stretch by a number, and add. Now then: the rent app. Every knob it owns is one of those two moves in plain sight.

So how does the app price a bathroom? It turns the columns into knobs. Morning knobs: 5 per bed, 3 per bath, 0 for total — thousands of taka. By evening it had quietly moved to a different set. Run both on every flat in the ledger, and keep your eyes on the rent.

<TwoKnobSets />

<Then>

<KnobShuffle />

The app can shuffle credit between the knobs — take $c$ off the bed and bath knobs, pile the same $c$ onto the total knob — and not one flat's rent moves. The total column spends only borrowed money: whatever credit it gets, it hands straight back. Two knob-sets, both fitting every flat perfectly, and no data can ever choose between them.

<SideQuest title="Multicollinearity: what statisticians call it">

Statisticians call this **multicollinearity**, and they're blunt about it: a serious app would refuse to quote a price, or quietly pick one set and hope. The dalal's app picked. Morning: 3000. Evening: −2000.

</SideQuest>

</Then>

<Step>

Enough of khatas for a moment — back to yesterday's remotes, and a test that splits buttons into the needed and the extra. Each button pushes Shiku some direction. The question: can he press real amounts and still end exactly at the door? Try the twin remote first, then the old one.

<ZeroWalk />

<Then>

On the twin remote, yes: $2u - v = (0, 0)$ — twice forward along u, once back along v, home without a wasted step. A set of buttons that can do that is called **linearly dependent**; there's an extra button in it. The old remote's e₁ and e₂ can't manage it, whatever you press. Those are **linearly independent**. And notice: it's a property of the pair, not of any single button.

<WalkHome />

</Then>

</Step>

<Step>

The dalal's own remote, then — the untidy one from the shop: u = (1, 2), v = (2, 5). No visible copy here; the buttons don't sit on one line. Independent, surely. Prove it the honest way: try to walk home first, then let the sums talk.

<NoVisibleCopy />

<Then>

Slot by slot, the machine proved what your fingers couldn't: $\alpha + 2\beta = 0$ forces $\alpha = -2\beta$, and then slot 2 kills it — $\beta = 0$, so $\alpha = 0$ too. Only the do-nothing presses land home. Independent. And notice what did the work: two lines of algebra, no hunting. When the ledgers grow — and in AI they grow enormous — this sum is the whole test, run by a machine.

<ThirdButton />

<Then>

However you drop the third button, the machine answers before it lands: press (−x, −y, 1) — e₁ and e₂ give back w's whole push, and Shiku walks home. On a floor with two slots, at most two buttons can ever be needed; from three on, one is always extra. And a floor with n slots holds at most n. Never more, however grand the remote looks.

<SpotTheExtra />

<Then>

The trap was the last page: bed and floor have no rule between them — two honest facts, neither one extra. Every other page had a column quietly reading the others' news. And that's the whole skill, worth keeping: before you trust a table, check whether one of its columns is only reading the others' news.

<Step>

## Try it

One last question from the dalal's own remote, before Abbu's pen comes out. The pair in question: u = (1, 3) and v = (2, 7). Extra button hiding in there, or two honest buttons?

<StretchReach />

<Then>

Slot by slot: $\alpha + 2\beta = 0$ forces $\alpha = -2\beta$; then slot 2 says $3 \cdot (-2\beta) + 7\beta = \beta$, and β dies — so α dies with it. Only (0, 0). “Looks like a copy” isn't a test. The sum is.

<TwoArrowsTest />

The arrows agree with the sum — they always do. A set is dependent exactly when one arrow lives on the line of the others. Two directions, genuinely two.

</Then>

</Step>
