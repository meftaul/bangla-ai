<Step>

Machine A's rule is an old friend of Fahim's: the haat's box. The query “fish” means $(1, 0, 0)$ — a 1 in the fish slot, nothing else. Run the box against each book.

<DotRanking />

<Then>

The thick book scored 20 — fair enough, it's stuffed with fish. But the boats-and-paddy book scored 3, and the fish note only 2. Yet the note is all about fish, and the book is almost not about fish at all.

<WordTiles />

Here the box is measuring how long the book is, not what it's about. Remember the coolie in 4.2? With enough strength behind it, a crooked push wins too.

<Step>

Machine B runs the same box, then divides by the two lengths. That is 4.5's cosine similarity. The query's length is 1, so really you only divide by the book's length.

<CosRanking />

<Then>

Both fish books scored nearly 1 — pointing exactly the fish way, whether the book is short or thick. And the boats-and-paddy book fell to 0.42, about 65° away.

<UnitFan />

Length here means “how many words were written”, and that has nothing to do with searching for fish. Divide out the length, and the books compete on only one thing: what they're about.

</Then>

Back home, Fahim found Mama and Mami in a hullabaloo over a new film. The film is called Hullabaloo, and its score is $(5, 5)$ — the most tears and the most laughs at once.

<LoudForBoth />

<Then>

With the box, Hullabaloo won for both of them. The tearjerker person and the comedy person got the same film! A film that wins for everyone doesn't win by fitting — it wins because its arrow is the longest, $\|(5, 5)\| \approx 7.07$.

<LongestArrow />

With cosine, each got a film of their own kind. Hullabaloo came second for both, because it points somewhere in between the two.

<Step>

So is the box always bad and cosine always good? Remember Samin's notebook at the end of 3.6. Length is sometimes the trouble, sometimes the real news.

Six jobs are coming. For each, say which measure it needs.

<LengthIsNews />

<Then>

There's just one question: **is length the real news here?** In a cow's price, the cow's weight is the price — divide it away and the answer is lost. In the van's total push, the strength is what matters.

<CowNews />

The last job is a bit cheeky. In the big streaming apps, the machine learns the film arrows itself, and a popular film's arrow tends to grow long. There, the box's pull towards long arrows is doing real work: a popular film gets shown to everyone a little more. But you'd choose that knowingly.

<PopularPull />

On the other hand, for searching books, matching word meanings, “show me more like this” — length is only noise. So in the searching done by search and chatbots, cosine runs the show almost always.

</Then>

</Step>

## Try it

<Check
  question="You're building a search for a newspaper. Some news is two lines, some is ten pages. What do you rank it by?"
  options={["The box — long news holds more", "Cosine similarity", "By the news' length, longest first"]}
  answer={1}
  hint="Why did the library's boats-and-paddy book climb to number two in a search for fish?"
>

Cosine similarity. With the box, a long article climbs on the strength of being long — even an article about something else, exactly as the boats-and-paddy book jumped over the fish note. Divide out the length, and the articles compete only on what they're about.

Those who picked the box — the argument isn't rubbish, long articles really do hold more. But a search isn't asking “which article holds the most”. It's asking “which article is about my subject”.

</Check>

<Step>

## The end! Apu kept machine B

Apu kept machine B. For finding fish, cosine — because length here is only “how many words”. If you bet on machine B, you won. But machine A didn't get thrown out either. Price, totals, popularity — wherever length is the news, the box is the right tool.

<KeepBoth story />

In one line: **if length is the real news, the box. If you only want what kind of thing it is, cosine.**
